A

Find your source, in this order New - added 30/08

Where to actually look - no filled-in example, because this step is search, not writing

  1. A Traditional Owner’s own statement, if you can find one. This is the strongest source type - a Darkinjung person or family speaking in their own words, not an organisation speaking on their behalf.
  2. Darkinjung LALC’s own materials next. Check darkinjung.com.au - their Culture and Heritage page, newsletters and annual reports. If nothing comes up for your issue, email or ring them directly and ask - contact details are on their site.
  3. AIATSIS next. aiatsis.gov.au holds broader research on Aboriginal land and water management that may cover your kind of issue, even if it isn’t Darkinjung-specific.
  4. An NSW Government Aboriginal affairs resource as a fallback. Search terms like ‘NSW Government Aboriginal cultural land management’ or ‘NSW Government Sea Country’ - these pages are a legitimate source type even when they aren’t Central Coast-specific.

Only these four source types count for your 5 marks. A general web search, Wikipedia, or a source outside this list won’t satisfy the rubric’s ‘accurate credible source’ requirement, no matter how well the paragraph around it is written. And if you do use Darkinjung LALC material, cite it as the LALC speaking for the community - not as a Traditional Owner’s own statement, since those are two different things.

B

Structure your paragraph New - added 30/08

The job each sentence needs to do - placeholders only, so there’s nothing here to copy

One Possible Sentence Frame

‘[Practice] is a way [Nation] people have looked after land or water, described by [Source, Year]. This has not been recorded for the Traditional Custodians of this site, but the area has a similar [landscape feature], so a similar approach may have been used here too. This would need to be confirmed with the relevant Local Aboriginal Land Council directly. If used here, it would help manage the site by [management benefit], and support Community wellbeing by [wellbeing benefit].’

  • Sentence 1: name the practice, who it belongs to, and cite it.
  • Sentence 2: say, honestly, whether it’s confirmed for Darkinjung Country specifically or not.
  • Sentence 3 (if not confirmed): explain - with a real reason, not a guess - why it might still apply.
  • Final two sentences: state a management benefit AND a wellbeing benefit, separately. One alone is enough for a C or B - naming both, clearly tied to the site, is what pushes an answer toward an A. See Step C below if this is the part that’s hard to write.

What actually separates a C from a B from an A New - added 30/08

Straight from the marking rubric, in plain English

  • For a C: name an example, and give one general sentence linking it to management or wellbeing, with a real source from the four types in Step A.
  • For a B: everything in C, but your example needs to be specific and named - a real named practice or type of knowledge, not a vague description like ‘Aboriginal people cared for Country’. And your benefit sentence needs to properly explain why it helps, not just say that it does.
  • For an A: everything in B, but with a second, separate sentence properly explaining the other benefit too - so both management and wellbeing are covered, each explained, not just one.

Stating vs explaining, since this is the main thing that separates C from B/A:
Stating (C): ‘This knowledge helps manage the site.’
Explaining (B/A): ‘This knowledge helps manage the site because [the specific reason this practice or knowledge actually does that - not just ‘it’s traditional’].’

One more thing worth knowing: the task sheet asks for 1–2 paragraphs for this whole section. A single sentence, even a well-written one, will read as underwritten - there needs to be enough there to actually fit the example, the honesty about what’s confirmed, and both benefits properly explained.

1

How it actually works New - added 30/08

The basics, before any tables or examples

APA uses an author-date system. That means every time you use someone else’s idea, fact or words, you put the author’s name and the year next to it, right where you used it.

There are two situations:

  • Paraphrasing - you’ve put it in your own words. You just need the author and year: (Smith, 2023)
  • Direct quote - you’ve copied the exact words in quotation marks. You need to add a page or paragraph number too, so the reader can find that exact spot: (Smith, 2023, p. 15)

And there are two ways to write the citation itself:

  • Parenthetical - everything sits in brackets at the end of the sentence: “...as shown in recent studies (Smith, 2023).”
  • Narrative - the author’s name is part of your own sentence, with just the year in brackets: “Smith (2023) found that...”

Both are correct APA - use whichever reads better in your sentence.

Frequently asked questions
Why do we need all those BOLTSS things on the map now?

Because a map without a scale or legend cannot be used to judge distance or understand what is shown. BOLTSS is the real convention geographers use so the map stands on its own.

What is the Aboriginal Custodianship section asking us to do?

Find one real example (a practice, piece of knowledge, or a Traditional Owner group’s own statement) connected to your issue or site, and explain how it supports managing that issue. Be precise about your source: your Local Aboriginal Land Council (Darkinjung for the Central Coast) is not the same as a Traditional Owner group’s own statement.

Where do I find sources for the Aboriginal Custodianship section?

Start with Darkinjung Local Aboriginal Land Council for the Central Coast, or AIATSIS and NSW Government Aboriginal Affairs resources for broader material.

Can I still do Wamberal Beach if everyone else is?

Yes, but you will need your own angle: a specific street, a specific proposed development, or a specific dataset, so your report is not the same as everyone else’s and reflects your own inquiry.

I want to use flooding as my topic, is that a problem?

It can be, because flooding primary data really does need a flood (or existing flood data) to work with. Either use existing records of past flooding (secondary data), or, if you know a king tide or heavy rain event is likely during your assessment window, plan your before-and-after photos and measurements around that date. This is still risky. “There was no flood” will not be accepted as a reason for missing primary data.

Do I really need three solutions? What if there is only one sensible one?

You need to identify and describe possible solutions, aiming for at least three. If you think there is only one good option, that is fine, but you still need to show you considered and ruled out the alternatives - that is what the evaluation is for.

Does my conclusion have to match what would really happen?

No, it should be your own well-justified recommendation based on your findings and evaluation criteria, even if the real situation is messy or contested.

Can I use ChatGPT?

For brainstorming a topic or finding search terms and sources, yes. Not for writing your analysis, conclusions or findings, and never as something you cite as a source of evidence. See the Referencing tab and AI Use tab for how to do this properly.

What if I cannot find much information about my issue?

That is a sign to pick a different issue or reframe it. Choose something councils or government agencies have studied and published on.

Is a photo enough for primary data?

No. A report missing genuine primary data (measurements, a survey, field observations) or missing secondary data cannot score above a D in Findings, no matter how many photos you include.

Do I have to visit my site more than once?

Yes, plan on at least two visits: one for original primary data, another once you have your secondary data and know what else you need.

What if I cannot get transport to my site?

Talk to your teacher early. If family cannot get you there, tell them what creeks, bushland or coastline is within walking distance, and you will work out a realistic option together.

Do I need to submit as PDF, or is Word okay?

Either is fine, if it is uploaded directly, not shared as a link.

What if I built my report in Canva?

Make sure your words are typed into an actual text box, not part of a picture. Test it: search your downloaded PDF for a word you can see on the page. If the search cannot find it, it is a picture of words, not real text, and it cannot be read or marked.

How do you make sure marking stays fair when you are marking so many reports?

Every report is marked against the rubric, and marking is also moderated.

Using Microsoft Forms for your survey
1

Create a new form

Sign in to your school Microsoft 365 account, open Forms, and select New Form. Give it a clear title, e.g. “Wattlebrook Creek Flooding Survey”.

2

Use rated or multiple-choice questions

Choice questions and rating scales (1-5) give you numbers you can actually cite - avoid open ‘what do you think’ questions, they are hard to turn into evidence.

3

Share the link

Use the Share button to generate a link or QR code, and send it to the people you want to survey (family, neighbours, local residents).

4

Read your results

The Responses tab shows totals and percentages automatically, and you can open the results in Excel for a chart or graph to include in your Findings.

Cite it properlyA structured survey with rated or multiple-choice questions is primary data - remember to cite your own figures in-text (e.g. “15 of 20 residents surveyed (75%)”) rather than just describing them in general terms.
Stuck on how to measure something?
1

Search beyond the Central Coast New - added 10/08

Just because your site is local doesn’t mean your ideas have to be. A school project in the USA, a university study in the UK, or a council report from another Australian state might all have tackled a similar issue to yours - search for how they measured it, then adapt their method to your own site. For example, if you’re studying coastal erosion, search something like ‘school project measuring beach erosion’ or ‘EMP dune erosion case study’.

MethodHow it works
Compare an upgraded site against a non-upgraded one New - added 10/08Find two similar spots where one has had management work done and one hasn’t, then take the same measurement at both - the difference gives a rough sense of whether the management actually worked. For example: dune height or vegetation cover at a beach with sandbagging or dune fencing installed, versus a similar nearby stretch without it; or pothole depth and road condition on a resurfaced section of a road versus an unresurfaced one.
Overlay historical fire dataFor vegetation regrowth studies, overlay a map of your site with fire history data (search for official NSW fire history mapping tools) to see how long ago the area last burnt, then compare regrowth to that timeframe.
