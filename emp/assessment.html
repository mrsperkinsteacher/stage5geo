What is this task?

Read this first before you begin

1

Choose a local environmental issue

‘Local’ means within the Central Coast (Gosford Council) area. Somewhere outside this area needs your teacher’s permission first, as it affects the Aboriginal Custodianship of Country section. You need to visit your site more than once for primary data collection, so local sites are usually easiest.

2

Decide the shape of your EMP

It can be a broad overview of the best way to manage a location (e.g. the best way to manage coastal erosion at Shelly Beach), or a topical study comparing specific options (e.g. is a seawall the most effective option to protect the Entrance Inlet).

3

Collect both primary and secondary data

Primary data is information you collect yourself - surveys, written observations, measurements, photographs. Secondary data is already published by someone else - ABS data, government reports, Council EMPs. Both are required in your Findings section.

4

Work through each tab in order

Each tab above is one section of your report, worth its own marks. Open a tab, read the steps, check the example responses, then write that section.

5

Submit as one PDF or Word document

Upload directly to iLearn - not shared as a Canva or Word Doc link - before 9pm on Monday 14th September 2026. All text must be real, selectable text, not an image or screenshot of text.

Text must be selectable, not an imageText built in Canva, screenshots, or slides exported as flat images cannot be read or marked. Every section of your report must contain real, searchable text.

Inquiry Question (Title)

Contextual understanding

/5
1

Choose a key inquiry question

It must name your issue, your location, and have an evaluative focus - not just a descriptive one. Example: “To what extent is wetland restoration the most effective way to manage flooding at Wattlebrook Creek?”

What makes a question evaluative, not descriptive?A descriptive question asks what or how something happens. An evaluative question asks how well, to what extent, or is X the best option - it sets you up to make a judgement, which is exactly what your Conclusion needs to do.

Referencing

Referencing and use of sources

/5
1

In-text citations for every source

Every fact, figure, quote or data point you use from someone else needs an in-text citation, right where you use it.

2

APA 7th reference list

Every in-text citation must match an entry in your reference list, and every reference list entry must be cited somewhere in the report. If you used AI for any part of this task, it needs its own reference list entry too.

Fabricated sources cannot be creditedMade-up, non-existent, or unverifiable sources score zero for that citation - regardless of how correctly they are formatted. If you are not sure a source is real, do not use it.
Worked example - a real APA 7 reference list

These are genuine, publicly available sources (nothing invented) - used both here and in the fictional Wattlebrook Creek WAGOLL examples elsewhere on this site - showing a website with no date, a dated report, a textbook, and how to combine two sources in one bracket.

Note: a source with no stated publish date is cited as “(n.d.)”. A source with a stated year, like the 2020 Central Coast Council report below, is cited with that year instead. Both are correct APA practice - the citation depends on what the source itself shows.

Note: when two different sources support the same point, cite them together in one bracket, separated by a semicolon, e.g. “(Central Coast Council, n.d.; NSW State Emergency Service, n.d.)” - see the Findings A example elsewhere on this site for this in action.

In-text examples:

“Central Coast Council (n.d.) provides flood risk mapping tools that show which properties are affected by different flood levels.”

“A 2020 floodplain risk management study for the Wyong River catchment (Central Coast Council, 2020) recommends a combination of wetland protection and stormwater upgrades to reduce flood risk.”

“The Central Coast is identified as a high flood-risk area (NSW State Emergency Service, n.d.).”

“Wetlands play an important role in flood mitigation by absorbing excess water (NSW Government, n.d.).”

“Wetland restoration is widely recognised as an effective way to reduce flood risk while also improving habitat quality (Swanson et al., 2026).”

“This is supported by both council and government flood data (Central Coast Council, n.d.; NSW State Emergency Service, n.d.).”

Reference list (alphabetical order):

Central Coast Council. (n.d.). Flood risk tools. centralcoast.nsw.gov.au/environment/flooding-central-coast/flood-risk-tools
Central Coast Council. (2020). Wyong River catchment floodplain risk management study and plan. centralcoast.nsw.gov.au (PDF)
Darkinjung Local Aboriginal Land Council. (n.d.). Culture and heritage. darkinjung.com.au/culture-and-heritage
NSW Government. (n.d.). About wetlands. Environment and Heritage. environment.nsw.gov.au/topics/water/wetlands/about-wetlands
NSW State Emergency Service. (n.d.). Central Coast - local information. ses.nsw.gov.au/local-information/central-coast
Swanson, L., Gray, N., Bowden, K., Harrison, A., & Newman, S. (2026). Jacaranda GeoActive 2: Stage 5 NSW Australian syllabus geography (6th ed.). John Wiley & Sons.
