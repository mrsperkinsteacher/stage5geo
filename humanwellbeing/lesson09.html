📋 Summary of Lesson

This lesson explained why climate change is called a threat multiplier: it doesn't just cause new problems, it makes existing ones - like poverty, hunger and conflict - worse. Lower-income countries contribute the least to greenhouse gas emissions but often suffer the most, especially where people rely on farming or herding.

The case study was the Horn of Africa drought. Oxfam estimates about 25 million people across Somalia, Ethiopia and Kenya will need food assistance by mid-2026, and in the worst-hit areas the price of water has risen by more than 2000%. You traced the drought cycle - failed rains, lost livelihoods, less money to prepare for the next drought - and looked at rising seas in the Pacific. Then you evaluated the humanitarian response: better early warning, but funding far short of what's needed.

📖 Glossary

Threat multiplier - something that makes existing problems, like poverty or conflict, worse, rather than causing them alone.
Food insecurity - not having reliable access to enough food for an active, healthy life.
Climate-vulnerable - especially likely to be seriously harmed by climate change, often because of poverty or reliance on farming.
Humanitarian response - organised aid that meets urgent human needs during a crisis.
Subsistence farming - growing food mainly to feed your own family, with little left to sell.
Poverty trap - a cycle where low income means low investment, which keeps income low.

💡 Did You Know?

In the worst-hit parts of Somalia, Kenya and Ethiopia, the cost of water has risen by more than 2000%. If a 20-litre jerry can cost 20 cents before, it would now cost more than $4 - out of reach for many families who have already lost their crops and animals.

🗺️ Where in the World?

Somalia - 6.5 million people facing hunger - nearly double early 2025.

Kenya - 3.3 million people affected and 15 of 23 dry counties at alarm level or worse (Feb 2026).

Ethiopia - part of the same drought region, with millions needing food assistance.

Tuvalu and Kiribati - small Pacific nations facing rising seas, despite contributing almost nothing to emissions.
