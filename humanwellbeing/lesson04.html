📋 Summary of Lesson

This lesson looked at life expectancy - the average number of years a person is expected to live in a given country - as one of the most widely used quantitative wellbeing indicators, because it reflects a population's overall health, nutrition and living conditions.

You traced the global trend since 1960 (around 51 years globally, rising to around 73 years by 2023), then looked at why that trend isn't even: high-income countries such as Japan and Australia sit above 83 years while some low-income countries such as Nigeria and Chad remain below 55 years, and even within Australia, life expectancy for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples sits below the national average. You finished by comparing Costa Rica and Russia on Gapminder - two countries with a similar income but a very different life expectancy - to explain that income alone doesn't fully predict health outcomes.

📖 Glossary

Life expectancy - the average number of years a person is expected to live in a given country.
Choropleth map - a map that uses colour shading to show how a value, such as life expectancy, changes from place to place.
GNI per capita - a country's Gross National Income divided by its population - in this lesson, used alongside life expectancy to see whether income and health actually move together.
Disparity - a noticeable difference or gap between groups or places.
HDI (Human Development Index) - a measure built from life expectancy, education and income combined.

💡 Did You Know?

Costa Rica and Russia have almost identical GNI per capita - about US$14,330 and US$14,250 - yet Costa Rica's life expectancy sits at 80.8 years compared with Russia's 73.4 years, a gap of more than seven years. It's a real, concrete example of why income alone doesn't fully predict how long people actually live - factors such as healthcare access, diet, sanitation and public health policy matter too.

📰 In the News

Global life expectancy estimates aren't a one-off figure - they're tracked continuously and updated roughly every two years in the United Nations' World Population Prospects report, and by the World Health Organization's Global Health Observatory. Keep an eye on either source if you want to see how the 2023 figures used in this lesson have changed since.

🗺️ Where in the World?

Costa Rica - life expectancy of 80.8 years on a GNI per capita of around US$14,330, the higher-life-expectancy half of this lesson's case-study pair.

Russia - life expectancy of 73.4 years on a GNI per capita of around US$14,250, almost the same income as Costa Rica but a noticeably lower life expectancy.

💼 Careers Connected to This Lesson

Epidemiologist - studies the patterns, causes and effects of health and disease within populations, the kind of work behind the life expectancy figures you used today.

Public health researcher - investigates why health outcomes such as life expectancy vary between and within countries, and what policies actually improve them.

Global health NGO worker - works for an organisation such as the World Health Organization on programs aimed at closing exactly the kind of life expectancy gaps you explored in this lesson.
